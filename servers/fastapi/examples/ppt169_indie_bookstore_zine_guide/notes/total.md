# 01_cover

Welcome to this issue’s zine culture guide. This is more than an introduction to independent publishing—it’s made in the language of zines itself: warm beige paper, misregistered spot-color headlines, and monospaced type with a photocopied feel. Over the next eighteen pages, we’ll start with a single sheet of paper, travel all the way to the doors of independent bookstores around the world, and return to your own desk. Ready? Let’s begin.

# 02_toc

Before getting into the content, let’s spend a minute looking at the structure of this guide. It has five sections, moving from the smallest concept to the biggest action: the first explains what a zine actually is; the second introduces its soul—the Risograph process; the third teaches you how to fold one yourself; the fourth takes you through independent bookstores around the world and in China; and the final section shows you how to browse, collect, and start making. The page numbers for each section are marked on the right, so you can start wherever your interest takes you.

# 03_zine_what

The first question: What exactly is a zine? The simplest definition is—a magazine minus commercial logic, minus editorial gatekeeping, and minus scale. A zine is therefore a self-made publication produced in extremely small quantities, with content entirely controlled by its author. It can be handwritten, collaged, a comic, or a manifesto. The key boundary is this: once a zine enters mass distribution and an editorial process, it is no longer a zine.

---

# 04_zine_history

zines are nothing new; they have a century-long history of waves. In the 1920s, Black creators self-published booklets during the Harlem Renaissance. In the 1930s, science-fiction fans began producing fanzines, and the term "fanzine" was coined by Russ Chauvenet in 1940. In 1976, punk ignited zine culture as photocopiers became widespread, with Sniffin' Glue becoming an icon. In 1991, Riot Grrrl pushed zines into third-wave feminism; Kathleen Hanna's Bikini Kill zine remains a paradigm to this day. Today, zine culture is experiencing a revival through book fairs and library collections—it has always been here, just in different forms.

# 05_risograph_origin

After the brief history of the zine, let’s look at its soul: Risograph printing. Risograph is a duplicator product launched in 1986 by Riso Kagaku Corporation, a Japanese company founded in 1946. It was originally created to provide an affordable, eco-friendly printing solution in postwar Japan, but decades later it became a core tool of independent art. Its four defining visual features—limited palettes, misregistered colors, halftone dots, and overprinted ink—are all technical limitations, but artists turned those limitations into a visual language. This page uses misregistration and halftones simulated in SVG.

---

# 06_risograph_process

The Risograph workflow actually takes only five steps. First, receive a digital image; second, burn the image into tiny pixel holes on a fibrous master; third, wrap the master around a colored ink drum, with each drum containing only one color; fourth, roll the drum to press the ink onto paper; fifth, switch drums and print again—multicolor prints are completed by passing the paper through multiple times. Each pass produces a one- to three-pixel shift. This is registration misalignment—in other printing processes, it is a defect; on the Risograph, it is a signature.

# 07_zine_folding

After all these concepts, let’s get hands-on. A single sheet of A4 paper can be folded into an eight-page zine in just four steps. First, fold it in half along the long edge, then along the short edge, and once more along the centerline to create eight rectangles; second, cut from the folded edge to the center point—just one cut; third, push both ends of the paper toward the center, and it will automatically fold into a small booklet; fourth, fill it with content—there are no rules. The only hard constraint is that the zine’s total page count must be a multiple of 4.

# 08_zine_content_types

So what exactly can you put in the “content”? Here are six common directions: sketches and comics, where ugly handwriting and crooked figures become part of the style; poems, manifestos, and short essays, with the Riot Grrrl Manifesto as a model; illustrated recipes or tutorials, the most widely accessible practical option; family photos paired with personal writing, where autonomy takes precedence over privacy; collages made from torn magazine pages and tape, whose materiality is something a photocopier cannot reproduce; and experimental visuals made purely of solid color blocks or textures — the absence of information is also a kind of information. Choose one or mix them all together; either works.

# 09_jimbocho

After discussing the methods, let's visit some bookstores. Our first stop is Jimbocho in Tokyo, one of the largest and oldest secondhand-book districts in the world. Between 130 and 180 bookstores are densely clustered around the intersection of Yasukuni-dori and Hakusan-dori, from the long-established Takayama Honten, founded in 1875, to Morioka Shoten, which opened only in 2015—the quirky shop that sells just one book each week. Utrecht in Harajuku also houses one of Japan's largest independent zine collections. In 2025, Time Out named this one of the coolest neighborhoods in the world. For book lovers, it is truly a place of pilgrimage.

# 10_three_cities

Next, let’s take in three cities in one go. Paris’s Shakespeare and Company has hosted thousands of tumbleweed writers since 1951, with just three conditions: read for an hour a day, write one page of autobiography, and help out in the shop — the bookstore here is a refuge for writers. London’s Daunt organizes its books by country, while Word on the Water is a Dutch barge converted into a bookstore and moored on a canal — here, the bookstore is an experience in which the setting is everything. New York’s Strand has 2.5 million books and calls itself 18 miles of bookshelves; Yu and Me Books, opened in 2021, is Chinatown’s first Asian women-owned bookstore — here, the bookstore is community infrastructure. Three stances, none right or wrong.

# 11_berlin

The final stop on our tour of independent bookstores in Europe: Bücherbogen in Berlin. Tucked beneath the brick arches of an S-Bahn viaduct, it sits beneath the rumble of passing trains, with shelves devoted to art, design, photography, film, and architecture. It doesn’t sell everything; it sells only these five subjects—and does them to perfection. This is the common model for independent bookstores in Europe: don’t be comprehensive; be absolute.

# 12_china_bookstore_map

Turn the lens back to mainland China. This map lists 15 independent bookstores with a zine sensibility, starting with the three most zine-centric destinations: Bananafish in Shanghai—the first art bookstore in mainland China dedicated to independent publishing, with its own Risograph studio; Fake Magazine in Ningbo—one of the earliest independent photography publishers in China; and Wild Dog in Zhengzhou—which carries more than 200 independent magazines from China and abroad. Below that are Yipian Old Shop in Zhoushan, Combo in Wuhan, and Protopaper in Xi’an—all hybrid spaces that also engage in independent publishing. Further down are bookstores focused mainly on international magazines or general literary selections. The list is not exhaustive, but follow this map around and the Chinese zine scene begins to take on a complete shape.

# 13_china_art_book_fair

If you want to see all these independent publishers in one go, go to an art book fair. China’s art book scene has two twin stars: abC Art Book Fair, founded in 2015 and held annually in both Beijing and Shanghai. Its sixth Shanghai edition brought together 145 Chinese art publishers and 41 international publishers, attracting more than 10,000 visitors. Close behind is UNFOLD Shanghai Art Book Fair, which usually opens a week after abC; its booth fees are higher, but so is its footfall. With the two fairs running across two consecutive weeks, they have become the most important annual physical pilgrimage for China’s independent publishing scene—Shanghai is worth a dedicated trip during these two weeks.

# 14_how_to_browse

If this is your first time visiting an independent bookstore, here are four suggestions. First, looking at the selection means looking at the owner: an independent bookstore is essentially a group of people’s mental bookshelf, and its selection is the owner speaking to you. Second, allow at least an hour; browsing itself is part of the product, and zoning out in front of the shelves is a legitimate form of consumption. Third, bring cash: some small shops do not accept electronic payments, and cash also reduces transaction fees. Fourth, ask before taking photos; most independent bookstores are sensitive about photography. One additional reminder: books from mainstream publishers can be bought anywhere. At an independent bookstore, you should be looking for independent publications and self-published artist books—that is the real point.

---

# 15_how_to_collect

Next, how to collect zines. There are four rules: First, start with one. Don’t set a goal of collecting a hundred from the outset; find one subject you care about most and let it snowball. Second, protect them with plastic sleeves after buying them. Zines are usually printed only once, and second printings are rare—one damaged copy means one fewer out-of-print edition. Third, follow your favorite authors and publishers on Instagram. New releases sell fast; once a hundred copies are gone, they’re gone. Fourth, attend abC and UNFOLD. Book fairs are where new releases launch and authors sign books. The secret to collecting for the long term is to set yourself a narrow anchor point—five years later, you’ll have a small museum dedicated to that subject.

---

# 16_action_checklist

If you’ve read this far, there’s one final step: make a zine yourself. Four steps to get started—one weekend is enough. First, choose a topic you genuinely care about. It doesn’t need to be grand; cats, breakfast, or your commute will do. Second, fold an A4 sheet using the method taught on page seven—it takes thirty seconds. Third, fill these eight pages; handwriting, collage, or photocopies of old photos are all fine. The first one should be rough. Fourth, print ten copies: give five to friends and send five to independent bookstores. The moment you send out your first copy, you become a zine publisher—the threshold isn’t commercial registration, an ISBN, or sales. It’s the second you send it out.

# 18_outro

One final thought—and everything this zine hopes to leave you with—is: print one, give one, trade one. A zine is the world's smallest publishing house, but it can also be your publishing house. You don't need to wait for credentials, an audience, or permission. One sheet of paper + one photocopier + one bookstore willing to stock it—the entire ecosystem is in place. The next zine is waiting for you.
