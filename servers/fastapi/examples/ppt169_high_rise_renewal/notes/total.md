# 01_cover

Hello everyone. Today I’d like to talk with you about a topic related to cities called active regeneration. The term comes from an article discussing what should become of aging supertall buildings after the high-rise fire in Hong Kong that shook the three regions on both sides of the strait. Starting with one fire, we’ll look at Sydney’s model, survey examples of adaptive reuse from around the world, and finally return to the Chinese cities where we live.

---

# 02_agenda

The presentation is divided into four parts. The first is a wake-up call, beginning with the Hong Kong fire and examining the systemic risks behind the aging of high-rise buildings. The second looks at a model: the Sydney skyscraper hailed as a “regeneration revolution.” The third is a tour of adaptive-reuse projects in Shenzhen, Shanghai, and New York. The fourth returns to China to discuss the truly tough challenge of aging high-rise residential buildings.

---

# 03_warning_fire

On November 26, 2025, a massive fire broke out at Wang Fuk Court in Tai Po, Hong Kong. All seven buildings affected were over 30 stories tall, nearly 100 meters high, and already 42 years old. The fire not only shook Hong Kong; it also served as a wake-up call for mainland cities undergoing urban renewal. Data from CNR are even more alarming: in just the first eight months of 2025, there were 36,000 high-rise building fires nationwide, already exceeding the total for all of 2023. Ground-based fire-rescue equipment can typically reach only 50 to 80 meters; beyond that, responders must rely on the building’s own fire-safety systems.

---

# 04_aging_highrise

Pull your gaze back a little, and you’ll see that this is really a form of structural aging. High-rise residential buildings that once symbolized the prosperity of urbanization begin to show concentrated risks in their structure, fire safety, elevators, and exterior walls after a decade or more. The burden of maintenance also grows increasingly heavy. One clear signal is that fewer people are willing to live in high-rises. The idea that “high-rise housing is the slums of the future” has already become a real cautionary lesson in some developed Western countries.

---

# 05_pruitt_igoe

The most classic cautionary tale is the Pruitt-Igoe housing complex in the United States. This model community consisted of 33 high-rise buildings and even received architectural awards when it was completed. But as public spaces went neglected and facilities fell into disrepair, affluent residents gradually moved out. The community slowly became home to lower-income residents and newcomers, while crime rates continued to rise. In 1972, all 33 high-rises were demolished by controlled explosions. From model community to demolition took just 18 years.

---

# 06_chapter_II

So here is the question: for an old high-rise, is demolition really the only option? That is exactly what Part Two will address. Let us turn our attention to the skyscraper in Sydney. Its answer is called “active regeneration.”

---

# 07_quay_quarter

This is Quay Quarter Tower, formerly the AMP Centre, built in 1976. It was originally destined for obsolescence, but the developer launched a program called "upcycling," turning it into the world's first comprehensively upgraded skyscraper. The core principle of the retrofit can be summed up in eight words: maximum retention, high-quality upgrading. In the end, 65% of the main structure and 95% of the core were retained. The design firm 3XN puts it plainly: the skyscrapers built in the latter half of the 20th century are gradually reaching the end of their design lives. We must extend their lifespans and unlock their latent value—that is the most fundamental definition of architectural sustainability.

---

# 08_eco_data

So where does the true weight of this regeneration lie? Three numbers. Construction waste was reduced by 80%, carbon emissions were 40% lower than with demolition and reconstruction, and energy consumption fell by more than 30%. It was precisely because of these three figures that, in October 2025, it became a finalist for the “Earthshot Prize,” launched by Britain’s Prince William, making it the first building ever nominated for the award. This tells us that old supertall buildings are not burdens: through scientifically guided renovation, they can be reborn and even become new urban landmarks.

---

# 09_shenzhen_mvrdv

Bring our gaze back home. This is the Shenzhen Women and Children’s Building, originally built in 1994 and redesigned under the direction of MVRDV. The most interesting design feature is a “colorful grid overcoat”: the facade gains nearly one meter of depth, providing shade while incorporating openable panels on the inside to promote natural ventilation and significantly reduce reliance on air conditioning. The renovated building combines a hotel, restaurants, a children’s discovery center, a picture-book museum, an infant and toddler care center, and a women’s and children’s theater, making it a landmark example of adaptive reuse in urban renewal.

---

# 10_shanghai_edition

Next is Shanghai. The building at 201 Nanjing East Road was built in 1988. Originally the East China Electric Power Building, it stands 126 meters tall and was the first high-rise on Nanjing Road after the founding of the People’s Republic of China. Its story spans 35 years: completed in 1988; renovated and expanded with No. 181 next door into The Shanghai EDITION in 2018; and upgraded for energy efficiency in 2023, becoming the first hotel in China’s urban-renewal sector to achieve carbon neutrality. The renovation introduced a complete suite of green systems, including high-performance windows, air-source heat-pump heat recovery, energy-efficient elevators, and water-saving fixtures.

---

# 11_one_wall_street

The third case takes us to New York. This is One Wall Street, a 1931 Art Deco skyscraper in Manhattan’s Financial District, designed by renowned architect Ralph Walker and rising 50 stories. It has now been converted from an office building into 566 apartments, ranging from studios to four-bedroom units. As we see on this tour, the vast majority of successfully converted high-rises were formerly office or commercial buildings. The reason is practical: these buildings generally have stronger structures than residential properties, offering greater flexibility and making it easier to introduce new uses.

---

# 12_chapter_IV

This brings us to the real challenge we must confront. Well-funded commercial projects with clear ownership are relatively manageable; retrofitting old high-rise residential buildings is the truly tough problem. Part Four: back to ourselves.

---

# 13_pilot_cases

There have already been several noteworthy experiments in China. The first is “Office Ring Orange” in Tiantongyuan, Beijing, which converted high-rise residential buildings into offices in an effort to address Tiantongyuan’s long-standing separation between jobs and housing. During the renovation, parts of the original beams and columns were reinforced, and the residential layouts were adapted into office layouts to meet the needs of small and medium-sized enterprises. The second is Zhejiang Gongxin Village in Hangzhou, the first old residential community in China to truly achieve “demolition and reconstruction on the original site.” Thirteen original buildings were demolished and seven new 11-story mid-rise buildings were constructed. The total reconstruction cost was approximately RMB 530 million, of which residents raised RMB 470 million themselves, contributing between RMB 100,000 and RMB 1 million per household.

---

# 14_real_difficulty

But replicating the “demolish and rebuild as-is” approach in ordinary high-rise residential projects is bound to be extremely complex. The reason is simple: high-rise communities have more households, making it incredibly difficult for all homeowners to reach a consensus. The matter of “getting every household to contribute” has left many renovation projects stalled. Scholars suggest that a possible solution lies in innovating governance models—for example, establishing “pension funds” for high-rise residences, or drawing on international experience to create neighborhood-committee-like organizations that allow residents to self-manage their buildings, starting with small-scale updates. Technical solutions are certainly important, but the key to unlocking the deadlock may lie in governance.

---

# 15_ending

Finally, let's end on a relatively optimistic note. This year, China is vigorously promoting the urban-renewal philosophy of “inspection before renewal.” As of November 2025, 297 prefecture-level-and-above cities and more than 150 county-level cities nationwide had launched urban inspections, focusing on issues such as hidden structural safety risks in residential buildings, illegal construction, and unauthorized alterations to load-bearing walls. From inspection to design, from coordination to regeneration, the future of supertall buildings lies in meticulous, fine-grained renovation. From pursuing height to building depth—this is a microcosm of urban development returning to rationality and pursuing high quality. Thank you, everyone.
