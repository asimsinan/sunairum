# 02_quote

Before getting to the main topic, let's hear something Müller-Brockmann wrote in 1981. He said: The grid system is an aid, not a guarantee. It permits many uses, and each designer can find a solution suited to their own personality. But one must learn how to use the grid—it is an art that requires practice. This sets the tone for today. The grid will not design for you; it simply gives you a framework for creating good design.

---

# 03_origin

Many people think the Swiss International Typographic Style emerged overnight, created by a handful of master designers. In fact, it was the result of six full decades of accumulation. In 1896, Berlin’s Berthold Type Foundry released Akzidenz-Grotesk, the first commercial sans-serif typeface in the modern sense. In 1908, the Basel School of Design completed its reforms. In 1918, Ernst Keller joined the Zurich School of Arts and Crafts, establishing the teaching principle of "form follows content." In 1936, 22-year-old Müller-Brockmann opened an independent studio in Zurich. Finally, in 1957, Helvetica and Univers were released in the same year. Rationalism did not appear out of nowhere; it was the fruit that grew over half a century from three intertwined threads: typefaces, schools, and teaching.

---

# 04_principles

So what are the core principles of this style? Müller-Brockmann summarized them in eight points. First, objectivity comes before expression — conveying information takes priority over the designer’s personal emotions. Second, the mathematical grid serves as the framework — columns, rows, spacing, and modules are all derived from basic units. Third, asymmetrical composition — rejecting the central authority implied by classical symmetry. Fourth, sans-serif typefaces. Fifth, flush left and ragged right, with even letter spacing. Sixth, objective photography replaces decorative illustration. Seventh, whitespace is structure, not leftovers — this is the most overlooked and hardest-to-achieve principle in the entire system, so we will devote a separate slide to it later. Eighth, it should be repeatable, teachable, and scalable.

---

# 05_figures

This system was not created by one person; it was built collectively. Ernst Keller laid its foundation in Zurich. Théo Ballmer introduced De Stijl's geometry into poster design, serving as a bridge between the two. Max Bill studied at the Bauhaus and later became the first rector of the Ulm School of Design, extending Swiss rationalism into product design. Emil Ruder was a central figure of the Basel School, and his book Typographie remains a bible of type design. Armin Hofmann was another pillar of Basel, and his influence continued through Yale. Müller-Brockmann was the central figure of the Zurich School; we'll discuss him in detail on the next page. The final two were type designers: Max Miedinger created Helvetica with Hoffmann in 1957, while that same year Adrian Frutiger created Univers.

# 06_brockmann

Let’s take a closer look at Müller-Brockmann himself. Born in 1914 in the small Swiss town of Rapperswil, about 40 kilometers from Zurich, he opened an independent studio in Zurich in 1936 at the age of 22, working across three fields: graphic design, exhibitions, and photography. The real turning point came in 1950, when he began designing the Musica Viva poster series for Zurich’s Tonhalle, using pure geometric elements—circles, arcs, lines, and rectangles—to express musical structures in abstract form. To this day, the series remains a textbook example of constructivist poster design. In 1957, he became a professor at the Zurich School of Applied Arts. In 1958, together with three like-minded designers, he founded the trilingual journal Neue Grafik, formally bringing Swiss ideas to the international design community. In 1981, he published Grid Systems in Graphic Design—the foundational text for today’s lecture. He died in Unterengstringen in 1996 at the age of 82.

# 07_typefaces

After the people, let’s look at three typefaces. Akzidenz-Grotesk was released in 1896 by the Berthold type foundry in Berlin—the first commercial sans-serif typeface in the modern sense. It predates Helvetica by a full 61 years and established the fundamental tone of sans-serif aesthetics. Helvetica was designed by Miedinger and Hoffmann in 1957. Its name literally means “Swiss,” from the Latin Helvetia. Neutral, objective, and exceptionally legible, it became the most widely disseminated typeface of the twentieth century. Also in 1957, Adrian Frutiger created Univers. For the first time, he systematized “family, weight, and width” through a two-dimensional numbering system with 21 weights, making it the ancestor of the modern type family structure. Within a single year, Switzerland produced two decisive typefaces—an eruption of an era.

---

# 08_grid_types

Müller-Brockmann categorized grids into four basic types. The first is the manuscript grid: a single full-width column, the oldest type, and virtually the only option for book text and long-form prose. The second is the column grid: multiple columns arranged side by side, with column widths, line heights, and spacing all calculable—the grammatical foundation of periodical and newspaper publishing. The third is the modular grid—a matrix of squares formed by horizontal rows and vertical columns, a powerful tool for posters and catalogs and the most recognizable grid type of Swiss Internationalism. The small red square in the lower-right corner marks the logic of the cover image used as the background for today's lecture. The fourth is the hierarchical grid: irregular but internally logical, used for composite layouts, magazine features, and web design. The common premise of all four grids can be summed up in one sentence: every distance is calculable.

---

# 09_modular

On this page, we make the abstract concrete. Choose a basic unit—for example, 16 pixels—and derive every other dimension from it. The 64-pixel safe margin equals four 16s; the 264-pixel column width is an integer multiple of 16 plus eight; the 32-pixel column gap equals two 16s; the 96-pixel line height equals six 16s; the 27-pixel body line height is 1.5 times the body font size; and the 32-pixel heading size equals two 16s. Once this basic unit is established, the entire layout becomes a calculable system. The diagram on the right shows the 4-column, 6-row modular grid used for every content page in this lecture—the two red-highlighted blocks are image areas, while the solid white highlights are text areas. Designers no longer “draw” layouts; they “build systems and place elements within them.”

# 10_negative_space

On this page, I want to pause and discuss the seventh principle—white space is structure. Look at the image in the background: 92% of the entire composition is pure white, with only a red circle and an extremely short black horizontal line. There is nothing between these two elements, yet you feel a powerful tension—where is the red circle, why is the black line so short, and why is it horizontal? That feeling is white space at work. White space isn't what's left over because the designer doesn't know what to put there; it carries information. One of the deepest principles of Swiss International Style is this: when you reduce the number of elements, those that remain are given greater weight. The space between is the message.

# 11_outputs

After style itself, let’s look at its influence. Beginning in the 1960s, Swiss rationalism spread in four directions. The first was corporate VI. Under Paul Rand, IBM, Knoll, Lufthansa, Olivetti, Braun, and a series of other major companies turned Swiss rationalism into an industry called “identity systems”; Müller-Brockmann himself served as an IBM consultant from 1967. The second was airport and subway signage—the New York subway’s 1966 Unimark design manual, Frutiger’s typeface for Charles de Gaulle Airport, and Otl Aicher’s work for the Munich Olympics. The legibility of public spaces is a direct extension of Swiss rationalism. The third was publishing, from Switzerland’s Du magazine to Eye, Domus, and Wallpaper, which remain models of cultural publishing to this day. The fourth was design education. Through Swiss-trained teachers at schools such as Yale, RISD, and Cooper Union, the Basel and Zurich teaching traditions passed this visual grammar down from one generation to the next.

---

# 12_digital_era

It took just twenty years for the style to jump from paper to screen, but the grammar remained unchanged. In 2008, Nathan Smith created the 960 Grid System: 12 columns, 960 pixels wide, bringing grid-based design systematically to the Web for the first time. In 2011, Twitter open-sourced Bootstrap. With the same 12-column structure, it brought modular grids to front-end developers around the world. In 2017, CSS Grid gained native support in all major browsers, making the modular grid a foundational part of the Web platform for the first time. By the 2020s, Material Design, Apple's Human Interface Guidelines, Adobe Spectrum, and Microsoft Fluent had extended major corporations' visual identities onto screens, transforming rule sets into component libraries. In a word—from printed posters to CSS Grid, the medium changed, but the same four elements remained: modules, columns, white space, and mathematical proportions.

---

# 13_brands

Look at the products you use every day, and you’ll find Swiss DNA everywhere. Apple is the most visible heir: from the Keynote presentations of the Jobs era to today’s Human Interface Guidelines, minimalist rationality forms the foundation of its product language. Google’s Material Design system brings Swiss engineering to mobile through an 8dp grid and geometric type. Spotify uses oversized type and grid-based blocks to turn music into a visual story—a continuation of the magazine aesthetic. Airbnb’s DLS design language system uses a modular grid and minimalist identity; even its photography follows an objective, documentary approach. IBM is direct lineage—Müller-Brockmann himself served as a consultant there in 1967, while Paul Rand took over its identity work around the same time. Lufthansa’s visual identity was created by Otl Aicher in 1962 and has remained largely intact for more than six decades. Half a century later, this grammar is still the default coordinate system for contemporary brands.

# 14_closing

Let me close with Müller-Brockmann’s most frequently quoted line: the grid is not a constraint. It is a grammar of freedom. If you take away just one thing today, I hope it is this: when a designer complains that the grid limits creativity, what they are really complaining about is that they have not yet learned how to use it; when a designer truly masters the grid, what they gain is not confinement, but a universal framework capable of supporting almost any content, style, or medium.
