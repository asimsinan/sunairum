# 02_Life Trajectory

[Transition] Let's begin with a timeline overview of Lin Huiyin's eventful life.

From her birth in Hangzhou in 1904, to her studies in the United States in 1924, and then her marriage to Liang Sicheng and return to China in 1928—her life in architecture began to unfold. Joining the Society for Research in Chinese Architecture, publishing her first paper on architecture, enduring wartime displacement, contributing to the design of the national emblem, and designing the Monument to the People's Heroes—every milestone reflects her resilience and talent.

[Pause]

A life spanning 51 years, capturing nearly half a century of China's most turbulent and glorious architectural history.

# 03_Early Architectural Practice After Returning to China

[Transition] Some question Lin Huiyin's legacy as an architect, saying she "left behind few works." But note that this was not due to a lack of talent—it was a consequence of her times.

In 1929, shortly after returning to China, she and Liang Sicheng completed their first project—a tombstone designed for her father, Liang Qichao. That same year, she contributed to the design of Jilin University’s Stone Building at the Liang-Chen-Tong-Cai Architectural Office. Most astonishing of all was the Jihai Railway Terminal: viewed from above, this Gothic building resembles a crouching lion, with its “tail” cleverly designed as a clock tower, symbolizing “China awakening like a lion.”

[Pause]

The vision and craftsmanship of a 28-year-old architect.

# 04_Architectural Ideals Amid War

[Transition] If the early years after her return to China marked the launch of her ideals, the war years were the most brutal test.

When designing the Geology Building at Peking University in 1932, she was so attentive to detail that she deliberately reduced the handrail size for women’s smaller hands—a tenderness toward the user rarely seen in architecture. In 1938, while designing Yingqiu Courtyard in Yunnan, she incorporated local materials and folk architectural wisdom. The Southwest Associated University campus design was “the most painful and aggrieving” project of her life: the plans were revised again and again, transforming a high-rise building into a thatched hut.

[Pause]

Yet in Kunming in 1940, she and Liang Sicheng designed the only home they ever owned. With its simple, elegant white adobe walls, it was the only house that belonged to the two architects in their lifetimes.

Key points: ① The challenges of wartime architectural practice ② Humanistic care revealed through the details ③ The uniqueness of the Kunming residence

# 05_National Cornerstone

[Transition] After the war, Lin Huiyin devoted the last of her strength to the nation’s most important buildings.

In 1950, she designed the main layout of the Babaoshan Revolutionary Cemetery. In 1952, she was appointed a member of the Architectural Committee for the Monument to the People’s Heroes. She proposed a creative revision: changing the steps from one tier to two, allowing the monument to better match the spatial scale of Tiananmen Square. Using Tang dynasty style as her model, she also personally designed the full set of decorative patterns and wreath reliefs for the monument’s base.

[Pause]

Later, she was buried at Babaoshan, which she had designed herself. Her tombstone bears only seven characters: Architect Lin Huiyin's Tomb.

Key points: ① Two nationally recognized architectural works ② Specific contributions to monument design ③ The deeper meaning of the epitaph

# 06_First Architectural Paper

[Transition] Beyond architecture, Lin Huiyin’s academic contributions were equally—if not even more—remarkable and far-reaching.

In 1932, at just 28, Lin Huiyin published “Several Characteristics of Chinese Architecture.” It was the first architectural theory paper published by a Chinese scholar. Before this, Western scholars viewed Chinese architecture as merely “a lone leaf” on the main branch of world architecture. Lin Huiyin rigorously challenged this prejudice, defining for the first time the fundamental characteristics of China’s timber-frame structure in theoretical terms.

[Pause]

Her daughter, Liang Zaibing, once recalled: “She was the central figure.” This was by no means mere praise.

Key points: ① A 28-year-old’s academic debut ② A rebuttal to Western prejudice ③ The historical significance of defining the Chinese architectural system

# 07_Overview of Academic Contributions

[Transition] The first paper was only the beginning; Lin Huiyin's academic vision extended far beyond that of her contemporaries.

She pioneered the concept of “architectural meaning,” arguing that architecture was not merely a combination of technology and beauty, but also a crystallization of history and human feeling. She wrote the introduction to Qing Structural Regulations, systematically outlining the framework of Chinese architectural theory. She was the first in China’s architectural field to advocate the preservation of vernacular buildings. In 1945, she published “Reference for Modern Residential Design,” presciently proposing that postwar architecture should be designed for ordinary people.

[Pause]

In 1949, she launched Tsinghua University’s first “Introduction to Housing” course, systematically teaching modern residential design—ideas that seem self-evident today required extraordinary foresight more than 70 years ago.

Key points: ① Five major academic contributions ② The originality of “architectural conception” ③ Foresighted academic vision

# 08_The Architectural Warrior Who Weathered War

[Transition] You may wonder: What kind of person was Lin Huiyin, exactly? The answer is—a strong woman.

Jin Yuelin sent her a poetic couplet reading “Beauty beneath the trees.” She dismissed it, saying: “What do I care about being a beauty? I still have so much to do!” When surveying ancient architecture in remote wilderness, if Mr. Liang dared to climb, Ms. Lin would climb too. She contracted pneumonia while fleeing amid wartime upheaval and never fully recovered her health, but she never gave up.

[Pause]

She taught her children to be “brave and fearless.” Despite her illness, she painstakingly wrote the history of Chinese architecture in remote towns. Her life contained something greater than the “small self”—the cause of architectural scholarship in China.

# 09_The Undisputed Architect

[Transition] At 28, Lin Huiyin once wrote to Hu Shi, candidly admitting that she had "not accomplished much" and "couldn't help but feel sad."

But let’s look at what she actually did—founding architecture departments at two universities, traveling deep into desolate regions to survey historic buildings, continuing to write architectural history during wartime, and devoting her final efforts to the national emblem and monuments. She was not only a pioneer who laid the foundations of the discipline, but also a trailblazer in thought.

[Pause]

If architecture was the cause to which she devoted her entire life, the results of that dedication have long surpassed her own concerns. She changed the way Chinese people see their own architecture.

# 10_Conclusion

[Transition] Finally, let’s return to that gravestone.

"Architect Lin Huiyin's Tomb"—seven characters that sum up a lifetime. Not the poet Lin Huiyin, not the talented Lin Huiyin, but the architect Lin Huiyin. Today, we have come to know her anew. She was China's first female architectural historian, a core member of the Society for Research in Chinese Architecture, a designer of the Monument to the People's Heroes, and a foundational figure in Chinese architectural theory.

[Pause]

In her 51 years of life, she showed us that women’s strength can transcend war, rewrite history, and raise a monument to a nation.
