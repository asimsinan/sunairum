# 03_zhanpao_decode

[Transition] Let's zoom in and decode this battle robe.

Its colors come from the traditional Chinese **Five Cardinal Colors**—**yellow**, taken from **Chinese smoketree** in Shennongjia, Hubei, representing the earth; and **cyan**, taken from **malan grass** in the early spring of Jiangnan, representing spring. After being boiled and allowed to settle, these two plants ultimately become colors in motion.

What is even more astonishing is its dyeing technique, called **"hand-pulled dyeing"**. Huang Ronghua dyes from below while Li Ziqi pulls the rope above, **raising it one centimeter every thirty seconds**. [Pause]

Centimeter by centimeter, the fabric slowly rises and falls in the dye, ultimately creating a natural transition reminiscent of ink wash. There is no room for the slightest mistake—this is a dual test of patience and skill.

Key points: ① The philosophical meaning of the five traditional colors ② The botanical origins of smoketree and indigofera ③ The precision and romance of dip-dyeing

# 04_history

[Transition] Plant dyeing was not invented by Li Ziqi's generation. Its history is far older than we imagine.

Historical records state: **"The art of dyeing dates back to the era of Xuanyuan. The Yellow Emperor established black caps and yellow robes, using the juice of plants to dye decorative patterns."**[Pause]

From the **Western Zhou dynasty**, when dedicated "dyers" were appointed, to the **Qin dynasty's** "Dyeing Office," then the **Tang and Song dynasties'** "Dyeing Institutes," and the **Ming and Qing dynasties'** "Indigo Office"—China's dyeing techniques gradually evolved from folk handicrafts into large-scale state-run production. Works such as *Essential Techniques for the Common People*, *The Exploitation of the Works of Nature*, *The Artificers' Record*, and *The Comprehensive Statutes of the Great Ming* systematically documented the techniques and processes of plant dyeing.

A dyeing river stretching back thousands of years still flows through Li Ziqi’s battle robe today.

Key points: ① The history of dyeing began in the era of Xuanyuan ② Five pivotal historical periods ③ Systematic records in ancient texts

# 05_natural_colors_1

[Transition] Next, let’s enter the world of specific colors. I’ve selected four representative traditional colors to share—starting with two “colors of nature.”

**The first is called “Rain-Washed Sky Blue.”** Its name comes from an imperial inscription by Emperor Huizong of Song: “**After rain, the sky turns blue where clouds break — make this the color for the future.**” These words established the supreme status of blue-and-white porcelain. [Pause] The dyeing method is 95% natural indigo overdyed with 5% baical skullcap, creating a translucent color touched with a hazy poetic quality.

**The second is called “Canghuang.”** It is the color of a spring-heralding bird and a young deer. The *Taiping Guangji* by Li Fang records a bird in the Gu Zhu Mountains, grayish yellow in color, that called out “Spring has begun” every year in the first and second lunar months. Tea pickers called it the “spring-heralding bird.” To produce this Canghuang color, use rhubarb with sappanwood.

Key points: ① Rain-washed sky blue — a color praised by Emperor Huizong of Song, and the color of blue-and-white porcelain ② Grayish yellow — the color of the spring-heralding bird ③ Cultural allusions behind dye formulas
Duration: 1.5 minutes

# 06_natural_colors_2

[Transition] Now let’s explore two more—**"Twilight Cloud Gray"** and **"Skywater Jade"**.

**Twilight Cloud Gray** is the dark purplish-red gray seen in the clouds at dusk. [Pause] Li Qingzhao wrote, “**The setting sun melts gold; twilight clouds form jade**,” while Liu Yong wrote, “Across a thousand miles, misty waves; dusk haze sinks deep beneath the vast Chu sky” — with a single gray, the ancients captured every emotion of twilight. The dyeing method is actually simple: sappanwood mordanted with blue vitriol and a small amount of green vitriol.

**Tianshui Bi** has a moving story. The consort of Li Yu, the last ruler of Southern Tang, once left incompletely dyed silk outside overnight. After absorbing the dew, the silk unexpectedly turned a vivid green. Li Yu named this accidental color **“Tianshui Bi”** — a single night of morning dew gave rise to a color name.

Key points: ① Twilight Cloud Gray—the dusk mood of classical poetry ② Skywater Jade—the unexpected beauty of a color formed by overnight dew ③ How the ancients named colors

# 07_poetic_colors_1

[Transition] If what I just discussed were “colors of nature,” the next two are more like “poetic colors”—they are not merely colors, but also carry literary imagery.

**"Silver red"** is a pale crimson, like the first tint of dawn or peach petals touched with frost. It appears repeatedly in *Dream of the Red Chamber*—that soft gauze came in only four colors: rain-cleared sky blue, autumn fragrance, pine green, and **silver red**, also called "rosy-shadow gauze." [Pause] The wash of silver-red rosy-shadow gauze before Daiyu's window is just like the rouge mark stained by the tears of the Crimson Pearl Fairy Grass.

**"Tuo red"** is the flush that appears on the face after drinking. Li Bai wrote: "**A beauty, on the verge of drunkenness, her cheeks glow red**." Tuo red evokes the scene of "the imperial concubine drunk on wine"—how utterly alluring.

Key points: ① Silver-red — the color of rosy gauze in Dream of the Red Chamber ② Tawny — the color of a beauty’s slight intoxication ③ The deep connection between color and literary atmosphere

# 08_poetic_colors_2

[Transition] Now let’s look at two other poetic hues—**"Daiqing"** and **"Acacia Gray"**.

**Daiqing** is a deep blue-green extracted from indigo plants. [Pause] Women in ancient China often used it to draw their eyebrows, so Daiqing always evokes images of beautiful women. In Wang Shifu’s *The Romance of the Western Chamber*, Cui Yingying is described as having “**blue-green brows drawn into a frown, a lotus face blooming with spring, and the beauty to topple kingdoms and cities**” — a single color brings an entire beauty to life.

**Lovesick Gray** is an imaginative color. [Pause] Li Shangyin wrote, “**Let not the spring heart vie with flowers in bloom; every inch of longing becomes an inch of ash**.” Longing burns to ash in the heart, unable to be dispersed or swept away — if colors can have feelings, Lovesick Gray is the most deeply emotional. The dyeing method uses tea leaves or pomegranate peel as the dye, mordanted with a small amount of green vitriol.

[Interactive] Has anyone noticed a pattern? When Chinese people name colors, they are never simply describing hue—they are telling a story and expressing an emotion.

Key points: ① Daiqing — the color ancient women used to paint their eyebrows ② Lovesick gray — a color name inspired by a line of poetry by Li Shangyin ③ The emotional foundation of traditional Chinese colors

# 09_dye_plants

[Transition] After discussing so many colors, you may be wondering—which plants are these colors actually dyed from?

The answer is actually quite simple: **the flowers, fruits, leaves, and stems can all be used for dyeing.**

**For red**, use madder roots, sappanwood stems, and safflower petals;
**for yellow**, use pagoda tree buds, gardenia fruits, and turmeric rhizomes;
**for blue**, use indigo plants and the leaves and stems of buckthorn;
**for black**, use walnut or chestnut shells. [Pause]

These four color families form the foundational palette of traditional Chinese dyeing. Madder, sappanwood, safflower, pagoda flower, gardenia, turmeric, indigo, walnut, chestnut… These names may sound ordinary, but once they enter the dye vat, they produce astonishing colors.

Key points: ① The dye plants corresponding to the four colors ② Flowers, fruits, leaves, and stems can all be used for dyeing ③ A “botanical list” of traditional dyeing

# 10_diy_dyeing

[Transition] What makes it even more wonderful is that plant dyeing is not a craft confined to museums; it is something ordinary people can try at home.

Five steps: **Boil a pot of water → Add the dye → Simmer for one to two hours → Strain out the dye bath and immerse the fabric → Finally, warm it in the sunlight to fix the color**. [Pause]

At a more advanced level, techniques such as mordanting, overdyeing, oxidation–reduction, and layered dyeing can create endlessly varied effects by building up different colors.

But what attracts me most about plant dyeing isn’t actually the technique—it’s **its unpredictability**. Its colors vary in depth, with some imperfect “flaws,” but every dyeing is one of a kind. [Interactive] This is something industrial dyeing can never achieve.

Dyeing the fabric by hand feels like having a conversation with the plants. **Feelings of tension and anxiety gradually disappear**. Here, you can see a quiet and carefree version of yourself.

Key points: ① A simple, practical five-step dyeing method ② Advanced techniques: mordanting, overdyeing, oxidation-reduction, and layered dyeing ③ Handmade “imperfections” are what make each piece unique ④ Dyeing as a process of self-healing

# 11_xuetaojian

[Transition] Let me share one final story—**Xue Tao Notes**.

Xue Tao, a gifted woman of the Tang dynasty, used water from the Huanhua Stream, the bark of the cotton rose hibiscus, and pounded hibiscus flowers to make a pulp, producing stationery in a color known as "hibiscus red." [Pause] This may be the most elegant splash of color in Chinese literary history.

Although squeezing fresh flowers can also produce hibiscus red, the yield is small and the process labor-intensive, making it impractical. According to Huang Ronghua's experience, **using sappanwood as the dye and alum as the mordant** produces the best results.

That is the charm of traditional colors—they are not specimens in a museum display case, but an elegance we can recreate with our own hands and experience for ourselves today. One sheet of Xue Tao paper, a tradition unbroken for a thousand years.

Key points: ① The literary allusions behind Xue Tao paper ② A recommended formula for modern reproduction ③ The “reproducibility” of traditional colors
