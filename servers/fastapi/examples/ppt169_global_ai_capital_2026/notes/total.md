# 04_q1_vc_landscape

Let’s look at the full picture for Q1 2026. Global venture capital reached $297 billion in a single quarter, an all-time high. The AI sector took 81%, or approximately $242 billion. The four major deals involving OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, and Waymo totaled $188 billion, accounting for 65% of global venture capital for the quarter. Crunchbase offered a succinct assessment: the money hasn’t increased—it has become more concentrated. That statement defines the capital climate of 2026.

---

# 05_hyperscaler_capex

If the three major model companies are raising funds in the foreground, then behind the scenes it is the four hyperscalers paying to build the infrastructure. Amazon: $200 billion; Microsoft: $190 billion; Google: $190 billion; Meta: $145 billion—for a combined $725 billion, up 77% year on year. But pay attention to a more critical structural figure: more than 60% of that $700-plus billion is flowing into power, cooling, and civil construction—not chips. Resource constraints have already shifted from “compute” to “power.” The next moat is gigawatt-scale electricity supply.

---

# 06_big_three_valuation_leap

Focusing on the three giants, let's look at their valuation jumps over six months. OpenAI rose from 300 billion at the beginning of the year to 852 billion in March, an increase of 184%. Anthropic rose from 61.5 billion to 380 billion in Series G and is currently discussing 900 billion, with a potential maximum increase of 1,364%. xAI rose from 50 billion to 23 billion, then, following its merger with SpaceX, saw its overall valuation surge to 1.25 trillion. Combined, the three companies' valuations rose from 411 billion to 1.46 trillion in six months—3.6 times higher.

---

# 07_valuation_vs_revenue_bubble

Plot valuation against ARR to see how much has been realized. The X-axis is annualized revenue, the Y-axis is valuation, and bubble size represents the number of investors. Every bubble sits above the fair-value line—valuations are running ahead of revenue. xAI is the furthest outlier, with estimated annualized revenue of just 5 billion but a valuation as high as 230 billion. Anthropic has the highest level of realization, with over 30 billion in annualized revenue. OpenAI falls between the two. The conclusion is clear: the company with the most extended valuation is not necessarily the one with the strongest revenue.

# 08_openai_investor_donut

Where did the money come from for OpenAI's largest-ever $122B financing round? Amazon committed up to $50B, or 41%. Nvidia contributed $30B, or 25%. SoftBank contributed $30B, or 25%. Microsoft, a16z, and others make up the remaining approximately $12B, or 9%. Note a structural observation: these three largest investors together account for 91% and are also OpenAI's largest customers—the investors and the buyers are the same group.

# 09_big_three_comparison

Put the three growth curves side by side. OpenAI is consumer-driven, with 900 million weekly active users, 24 billion in annualized revenue, and enterprise revenue already accounting for 40%—a consumer machine sprinting toward an IPO. Anthropic is enterprise-driven, with over 30 billion in annualized revenue and 40 billion in cumulative commitments from Google. Most importantly, its valuation is reportedly already ahead of OpenAI’s—900 billion versus 852 billion. xAI is built around a merger narrative: a 230 billion valuation plus SpaceX’s overall 1.25 trillion valuation, with Tesla putting in 2 billion. Combined, the three are worth 2.48 trillion, already more than two-thirds of Apple’s market cap over the same period.

# 10_china_five_ranking

The lens turns to China. DeepSeek has secured the first lead investment from the national Big Fund, with a valuation of 50 billion, making it the true leader of China’s top AI tier. Zhipu’s Hong Kong-listed market cap is 56.1 billion, and MiniMax’s is 37.2 billion—both reflecting secondary-market pricing after their Hong Kong listings in January. Moonshot has a private-market valuation of 20 billion, with a 2 billion single-round investment—the largest in the history of China’s foundation models. StepFun is negotiating at around 8 billion. One data caveat: Hong Kong-listed market caps and private-market valuations cannot be compared directly; only their order of magnitude can be assessed.

---

# 11_us_china_dual_track

U.S.–China AI capital has become structurally differentiated. The U.S. relies on mega-sized private placements, with individual rounds ranging from 30 billion to 122 billion; China relies on smaller private placements plus IPOs, with individual rounds ranging from 2 billion to 4 billion. U.S. IPOs have been delayed, while China brought Zhipu and MiniMax to the Hong Kong stock market as early as January. The U.S. is market-driven, while China is led by major funds. Peak valuations reached 852 billion in the U.S. and 56.1 billion in China. The two structures have largely solidified in 2026, and the divergence in their paths is no longer reversible.

# 12_second_tier

The second tier consists of four elite companies valued between $1 billion and $5 billion. SSI was founded by Sutskever, and its valuation soared from $5 billion to $32 billion in six months, driven entirely by its people and the narrative around its direction—with no product yet. Perplexity is valued at $21 billion, has 45 million monthly active users, and is taking on Google Search head-on. Mistral, valued at $13.7 billion, represents Europe’s sovereign open-source AI and is building a data center in Paris with 13,800 Nvidia GPUs. Cohere and Aleph Alpha have merged into a $20 billion-valued challenger to the transatlantic sovereign enterprise stack. Together, the four are worth $86.7 billion—just one-tenth of OpenAI’s valuation at the same time.

# 13_chapter_nvidia

Now we enter Part Five. This chapter examines a core structural problem: when a chipmaker is also a major shareholder. Nvidia's equity investments have exceeded $40 billion since 2026, with $30 billion going to OpenAI and investments in xAI, Anthropic, and Mistral all in place. At the same time, 85% of its revenue comes from just six customers. This is a closed loop.

---

# 14_nvidia_sankey

Map Nvidia's circular investment as a flow of funds. On the left, Nvidia invests $40 billion in equity. In the middle, $30 billion goes to OpenAI, approximately $3 billion to xAI, approximately $2 billion to Anthropic, approximately $1 billion to Mistral, and $4 billion to CoreWeave and others. On the right, these AI companies then funnel most of the money back to Nvidia as multiyear commitments to purchase chips—more than $100 billion is expected to flow back. A Wedbush analyst's comment is exceptionally precise: this is exactly the circular-investment theme the market is most concerned about.

---

# 15_nvidia_pareto

A Pareto chart of Nvidia's customer concentration. Microsoft ranks first at 18%, Meta second at 16%, Google third at 15%, and Amazon fourth at 11%—the top four account for 60% combined. Add OpenAI and CoreWeave, and the top six reach a cumulative 85%. This is a twofold risk: if any hyperscaler cuts capital spending, it could deal a cascading blow to Nvidia's earnings and, in turn, the entire AI supply chain.

---

# 16_stargate_hub

The Stargate project is the physical foundation of it all. OpenAI, Oracle, and SoftBank are investing $500 billion in total, with 10 GW of capacity over four years. Its central hub is the operational flagship in Abilene, Texas, which will reach 1 GW by mid-2026. Around it are seven sites: Shackelford, TX; Doña Ana, NM; Saline, Michigan; the undisclosed Midwest site; Lordstown, Ohio; and Milam, Texas. So far, 7 GW has been planned, with $400 billion to be invested over the next three years. GB200 racks have already been delivered to Abilene. Sam Altman put it simply: “up and running.”

# 17_chapter_bubble

Now we enter the final section: the bubble. Hyperscaler capital expenditure has reached $400 billion, while enterprise AI revenue is only $100 billion. The 46-percentage-point growth gap is 1.5 times the 32-percentage-point gap during the 2001 telecom bubble—in other words, the gap has already surpassed that of the 2001 telecom bubble. An MIT study shows that 95% of generative AI pilots have produced no business value. This is the real data behind the bubble.

---

# 18_capex_vs_revenue

A scissors chart. The red line shows hyperscaler capital expenditure, rising from $20 billion in 2020 to $725 billion in 2026—almost a vertical takeoff. The green line shows enterprise AI revenue, growing from several billion dollars in 2020 to approximately $100 billion in 2026, with a much gentler slope. The shaded area between the two lines is the ROI chasm. Capitalism is patient, but its patience is limited.

# 19_four_risks_quadrant

Four major risks, amplifying one another. Top left: the capex–revenue gap has surpassed that of the 2001 telecom bubble. Top right: MIT data shows that 95% of enterprise AI pilots have generated no commercial value. Bottom left: 85% of Nvidia’s revenue comes from six customers, with nearly 60% from its top four. Bottom right: financing structures are shifting from operating cash flow toward debt financing. Any one of these risks would be manageable on its own, but all four are now present simultaneously, amplifying one another.

Six key judgments to close. First, capital concentration is the defining theme of 2026. Second, computing power is the real hard currency. Third, the next moat is electricity, not chips.
